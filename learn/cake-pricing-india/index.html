How to price a homemade cake in India

Start with the cost of the complete order, including your time. Then compare a cost-based price with what the actual order requires and what your customers are willing to pay.

Written by PromptPack, the seller of BakePrice Studio. The INR figures below are fictional arithmetic examples, not surveyed ingredient prices, wage guidance or typical bakery earnings.

1. Define the recipe and the customer quantity

Choose one unit and keep it consistent: a cupcake, a cookie, a box or a whole cake. If a batch makes 24 cookies but the order is recorded in boxes of six, convert both quantities to the same unit before calculating. A cake's weight alone does not describe the time needed for a custom finish.

2. Cost the amount of each ingredient used

For each ingredient, divide the purchase cost by the pack quantity, then multiply by the recipe usage. A 1,000 g flour pack costing ₹60 contributes ₹15 to a recipe using 250 g. Pack and recipe quantities must share a unit. Do not divide a price per kilogram by a usage in grams without converting one of them.

Use your own purchase records. Include extra materials and test-bake allocations where appropriate. This guide supplies no food-safety, nutritional or allergen assessment.

3. Account for whole batches and your time

Round up order quantity ÷ recipe yield to cost whole batches when that matches how you produce. Add hands-on hours × your chosen hourly rate. The calculator scales batch hours with batches; consultations or extra custom decoration may require additional order-level labour outside this exercise.

Owner labour and the amount remaining after recorded costs are different things. Paying yourself for work is a cost decision; a chosen margin is not another guaranteed wage.

4. Add packaging, overhead and omitted order costs

Include boxes, boards and other packaging using your actual consumption. Allocate overhead consistently, without adding the same electricity or equipment cost twice. Review delivery, payment fees, taxes and other order-specific costs separately. A cost you leave out cannot appear in the resulting price scenario.

A whole-batch example in INR

Fictional cupcake order: one recipe makes 12, but the customer orders 14. Two whole batches produce 24, leaving 10 spare units. This example allocates both batches to this order; it assumes no income from spare units.

For a 25% target margin on these included costs: 1,000 ÷ (1 − 0.25) = 1,333.33, rounded for display. That is a cost-based scenario, not a market price or a profit guarantee. Payment fees, taxes, delivery, extra order hours and any other omitted costs still need review.

5. Keep margin separate from markup

With ₹1,000 of included costs, adding a 25% markup produces ₹1,250. The ₹250 difference is 20% of that selling price. A 25% target margin on the same costs instead gives 1,000 ÷ 0.75 = 1,333.33 before rounding and omitted expenses.

Margin uses the selling price as its denominator; markup uses cost. See Shopify's explanation of margin and markup. The example here is independently calculated and does not prescribe a suitable percentage for your business.

6. Review the actual quote before sending it

  • Confirm product, size, quantity, finish, delivery and what is included.
  • Reconcile ingredient costs, labour and packaging with the actual order.
  • Check extra order costs and applicable taxes or fees with appropriate advice.
  • Choose the final customer price; the calculator does not measure local willingness to pay.
  • State the deposit and balance clearly. A deposit reduces what remains payable; it does not reduce the agreed quote total.
  • Compare recorded costs and time after fulfilment to improve the next quote.

Common pricing questions

Should I multiply ingredient cost by three?

A fixed multiple can miss different labour and packaging requirements. Cost the actual recipe and work, then assess the final quote against the order and your market. This guide does not prescribe a universal multiplier.

Does the scenario include GST or payment fees?

No. The public exercise includes only its named input costs. Review tax treatment, fees and other omissions separately; a target on partial costs is not a net margin.

Can I charge less if I expect to sell the spare cupcakes?

Do not assume those sales will happen. This whole-batch example assigns batch costs to the quoted order. Any shared-production allocation should reflect a separately reviewed plan and actual outcomes.
